Projects in the region often depend on materials that cross two or three borders before they reach site. Most delays here are paperwork and waiting, not distance.

## Start from the date the site needs the material

Work backwards from the installation date, not forwards from the order. Add the supplier lead time, the production slot, transport, customs and a buffer, then look at the total. If it is longer than the schedule allows, you have found the problem on day one.

## Documents are part of the route

- Confirm the harmonised system code for every item before ordering.
- Agree who is the importer of record at each border.
- Keep one shared folder with certificates of origin, packing lists and invoices that match each other exactly.

## Choose the mode and the port together

Road is flexible; rail is cheaper for volume; sea needs port slots. Compare total cost _and_ the variability of arrival time. A cheaper route that arrives within a ten-day window may cost more than a dearer one that arrives within two.

## Hold a buffer where it is cheapest

A small stock at the site is costly. A larger stock in a bonded warehouse near the port is often cheaper and gives you room to react.

Planned early, freight stops being a surprise and becomes a line in the schedule.
