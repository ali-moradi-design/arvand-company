When a large project finishes late, the cause is rarely a single dramatic event. Looking back across our engagements, the delay was usually baked in during the first month, when the schedule was drafted, accepted and rarely questioned again.

## The optimistic baseline

A baseline is a promise. If it assumes that approvals arrive in two weeks, that every contractor mobilises on the same day and that long-lead equipment ships without a hiccup, it is not a plan. It is a wish with dates on it.

## Three checks that catch problems early

- **Trace the critical path to a person.** Every activity on it needs a named owner who has confirmed the duration in writing.
- **Price the float.** If an activity has ten days of float, ask what it would cost to use them. Float that nobody can afford to use is not float.
- **Test the first ninety days.** Walk through the first quarter with the people who will do the work. Their objections are worth more than a software report.

## What to do on day one

Set up weekly reporting before the work starts, and agree what a _variance_ is. A report that only says "on track" until the week things break teaches everyone to stop reading it.

> A late project is rarely a surprise to the people on site. It is a surprise to the people who were never told.
